# 6. What we'd do next

1. **Register-blocked micro-kernel.** §5.1 shows the kernel is limited by fixed costs per row. A BLAS-style micro-kernel holding a small block of C in registers would raise $F_{\max}$ and remove T₀. Blocked would gain on every flop and Strassen only on its leaves, so the crossover should move to larger n. How far is the central Theme 3 question: our crossover (about 160) is low precisely because our kernel is modest.
2. **Separate the two alignment effects.** E9 fixed the vector remainder and the 32-byte row alignment together. Padding the temporaries' strides to multiples of 4 while keeping odd leaf widths would show which of the two matters.
3. **Remove the carry-over bias, then find its cause.** Randomise or Latin-square the order of variants instead of rotating it, or insert a neutral flush workload between variants. Then use hardware counters (AMD uProf) on E9b to see which state carries over: prefetcher, TLB or branch predictor.
4. **Winograd's variant, hybrid depth and threads.** Winograd's form (Winograd, 1971) needs 15 additions instead of 18, attacking the residual in Figure 10. A per-level recursion choice could exploit the leaf-shape effect (§5.2). Strassen's seven independent products also parallelise naturally, which would make E6's cache pressure the normal condition.
5. **Open questions from the data.** Why does allocation cost peak at n = 512 (§4.5)? Why does Strassen's componentwise error fall with n on wide-range inputs (§4.6)?
