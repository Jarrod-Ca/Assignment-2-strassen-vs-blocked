# 6. What we'd do next

1. **Register-blocked micro-kernel.** §5.1 shows the blocked kernel is limited by fixed costs per row, not by memory. A BLAS-style micro-kernel that keeps a small block of C (for example 4 × 8) in registers would raise F_max and remove T₀. Both algorithms would speed up, but blocked would gain on every flop while Strassen gains only on its leaves. So we expect the crossover to move to larger n. How far is the central open question of Theme 3: our crossover (about 160) sits well below published ones precisely because our kernel is modest.
2. **Separate the two alignment effects.** E9 fixed the vector remainder and the 32-byte row alignment together. Padding the temporaries' strides to multiples of 4 while keeping odd leaf widths would show which of the two matters.
3. **Remove the carry-over bias, then find its cause.** Randomise or Latin-square the order of variants instead of rotating it, or insert a neutral flush workload between variants. Then use hardware counters (AMD uProf) on E9b to see which state carries over: prefetcher, TLB or branch predictor.
4. **Winograd's variant and hybrid depth.** Winograd's form of Strassen needs 15 additions instead of 18, attacking the 15% residual in Figure 10. A per-level choice of whether to recurse, rather than one global cutoff, could also exploit the fact that the kernel rate depends on leaf shape (§5.2).
5. **Multi-threading.** Strassen's seven independent products parallelise naturally. With eight cores competing for one L3, the cache-pressure behaviour of E6 becomes the normal operating condition rather than an experiment.
6. **Open questions from the data.** Why does allocation cost peak at n = 512 (§4.5)? Why does Strassen's componentwise error fall with n on wide-range inputs (§4.6)?
